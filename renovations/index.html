Preparing Holley Hall and Lawrence Memorial Library for Bristol’s next chapter

In 2026 Bristol renovated Holley Hall and the Lawrence Memorial Library at the same time, with the same architect, the same builder and grant money that has to be spent by the end of the year. Holley Hall is essentially finished. At the library, testing turned up a chemical cleanup that was not in the plan. This page tells the story of both.

Inside the Holley Hall retrofit

March to September 2026

A 2010 renovation insulated Holley Hall's lower level and attic but left the upstairs walls alone: a single layer of brick over wood framing with little or no insulation, single-pane windows and uninsulated doors. One oil boiler heats the whole building, and it still does. The lower level also has heat pumps in each office area and the conference room, with heat recovery ventilation. The upper floor had no mechanical ventilation beyond bathroom fans and no cooling. The 2024 energy assessment summary noted that the hall "becomes very uncomfortable in hot weather."

In March crews stripped the hall walls to the framing. They sheathed and taped them for air sealing, blew in cellulose, added a layer of wood-fiber insulation board and refinished the walls. Two ducted heat pumps now serve the hall, with a third in the tower office, and two ventilators under the stage bring in fresh air while keeping most of the heat. Fifteen windows were repaired and fitted with interior storm windows, exterior doors were weatherstripped, programmable thermostats went in, and LED lights went in under the balcony.

Is the building tighter?

How to read this. A blower door test uses a fan to pull air out of the building and measures how much leaks back in. Lower is better. The mid-project test came before every air barrier was finished. The final test on August 10, after the work was complete, measured 58% less leakage than before the project. Per square foot of building shell, the hall went from 1.00 cfm, which the 2024 assessment rated "extremely leaky," to 0.42 cfm.

Inside the Lawrence Memorial Library renovation

Spring to December 2026

The library project was always the bigger one. The plan swaps the oil boiler for propane, adds heat pumps and fresh-air ventilation with better filters, insulates the upper walls and brings the attic to R-60. It turns a storage room into a soundproofed quiet room, updates the electrical, deals with lead paint, repairs rotted trim and siding and rebuilds the front porch and steps.

Most of that went the way renovations go. The attic was insulated in April, the outdoor heat pump units went on their stands the same month, and the worn front steps were rebuilt by late July.

Then came the PCBs

Before the inside work went further, the building was tested in April for asbestos, lead and PCBs, industrial chemicals banned in 1979 that still turn up in older materials. Asbestos was found only in the first-floor plaster, and the work there was cleaning up dust and debris, finished by mid-July. The brown glue under the first-floor carpet held PCBs at 140 mg/kg. Federal rules require anything over 50 to be removed as PCB waste, so the carpet, the glue and the wood floor under it came out of the main first-floor rooms.

After removal, a second firm takes samples to confirm the building is clean. The standard is 1 mg/kg. It took three rounds to get there.

The first round showed the PCBs had soaked into the wood subfloor itself, so that came out too. By August 21 those rooms were down to their joists, with the radiant heating tubes still in place and the space sealed in plastic sheeting. In places you could see straight through to the ceiling of the rooms below.

    Keeping the library open

    The library closed its North Street building to the public on March 10, 2026, expecting to reopen at the beginning of May while exterior work continued. Then asbestos turned up in the wall plaster and PCBs in the carpet adhesive. An eight-week closure is now heading toward nine months. Library staff kept the library running the whole time, first by email and with programs held off-site, then from a pop-up branch in the BristolWorks building, while also packing up the first floor so the cleanup could go ahead.

    What it all costs As of

    Both grants pay by reimbursement: the Town pays the bills and the State pays it back. What the grants do not cover falls to the Town. For the FY2027 budget, the Town raised its yearly contribution to the Capital Building and Maintenance Fund to $120,000, from a typical $30,000, in part to cover these two projects.
