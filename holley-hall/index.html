Inside the Holley Hall retrofit

From March to September 2026 the upper floor of Holley Hall was a construction site while crews insulated its walls, sealed its windows and added heat pumps and fresh-air ventilation. A state energy grant paid for most of it. This page covers what was done, why, who did it, what it cost and what is still open.

The building 1884 to today

Holley Hall was designed by architect C.W. Damon of Haverhill, Massachusetts, and completed in November 1884. It has served as Bristol's town hall ever since. The Bristol Cornet Band was the first group to perform in the hall, in January 1885, and it has hosted plays, dances, concerts and craft shows in the years since. The building is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

Today it holds the Town offices, Town Meeting, Selectboard meetings, a polling place and Recreation Department programs, and it is a designated emergency shelter. Volunteers built an accessible ramp on the west side in 1991. A 2010 renovation corrected high radon levels, renovated the first-floor offices, repaired the roof and the hall's balcony, and added a stair and lift tower on the west side. A Cultural Facilities grant paid for new stage lighting and a movie screen at the same time.

Why the work was done From the 2024 energy assessment and scope of work

Holley Hall is a historic two-story brick building of about 7,800 square feet that serves as Town office and community space, with a large meeting hall and stage on the second floor. A 2010 renovation insulated the lower level and the attic well, but the upper-floor walls were left alone: a single layer of brick over wood framing with little or no insulation, single-pane windows and uninsulated doors.

The whole building is heated by one oil-fired boiler, and that is still the case. The lower level also has heat pumps in each office area and the conference room, along with heat recovery ventilation (HRV). The upper floor had no mechanical ventilation and no cooling, so the hall was cold and drafty in winter and, in the energy assessor's words, "very uncomfortable in hot weather." The assessment also pointed out that heat pumps would let the hall serve as an emergency heating and cooling center.

Is the building tighter? Blower door tests, air leakage at 75 pascals

How to read this. A blower door test depressurizes the building with a fan and measures how much air leaks in. Lower is better. The mid-project test was taken before every air barrier was finished. The final test on August 10, 2026, after the work was complete, measured less leakage than before the project. Per square foot of building shell, the hall went from 1.00 cfm, which the 2024 assessment rated "extremely leaky," to 0.42 cfm. The consultant places that in the range of average construction, close to the 0.30 cfm reference Vermont's commercial energy code uses for new buildings.

What it cost

The State's Municipal Energy Resilience Program grant paid for most of the work. It required no local match, and the State paid the full award in five payments between February and August 2026. The Town covers the rest: it set aside a $150,000 reserve for the project, and costs have now passed that reserve too.
