Inside the Holley Hall retrofit

From March to September 2026 the upper floor of Holley Hall was a construction site while crews insulated its walls, sealed its windows and added heat pumps and fresh-air ventilation. A state energy grant paid for most of it. This page covers what was done, why, who did it, what it cost and what is still open.

The building 1884 to today

Holley Hall was designed by architect C.W. Damon of Haverhill, Massachusetts, and completed in November 1884. It has served as Bristol's town hall ever since. The Bristol Cornet Band was the first group to perform in the hall, in January 1885, and it has hosted plays, dances, concerts and craft shows in the years since. The building is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.

Today it holds the Town offices, Town Meeting, Selectboard meetings, a polling place and Recreation Department programs, and it is a designated emergency shelter. Volunteers built an accessible ramp on the west side in 1991. A 2010 renovation corrected high radon levels, renovated the first-floor offices, repaired the roof and the hall's balcony, and added a stair and lift tower on the west side. A Cultural Facilities grant paid for new stage lighting and a movie screen at the same time.

Why the work was done From the 2024 energy assessment and scope of work

Holley Hall is a historic two-story brick building of about 7,800 square feet that serves as Town office and community space, with a large meeting hall and stage on the second floor. A 2010 renovation insulated the lower level and the attic well, but the upper-floor walls were left alone: a single layer of brick over wood framing with little or no insulation, single-pane windows and uninsulated doors.

The whole building is heated by one oil-fired boiler, and that is still the case. The lower level also has heat pumps in each office area and the conference room, along with heat recovery ventilation (HRV). The upper floor had no mechanical ventilation and no cooling, so the hall was cold and drafty in winter and, in the energy assessor's words, "very uncomfortable in hot weather." The assessment also pointed out that heat pumps would let the hall serve as an emergency heating and cooling center.

History How the building got here

Holley Hall has been Bristol's town hall since 1884. It was built on land given by Cornelia Holley Smith, on the corner where her father's store burned in 1876, and has served as a theater, a movie house, a basketball court, a jail, a schoolhouse and the town offices. The 2026 energy work is the latest chapter in a long run of repairs.

Building Holley Hall

1884 · 4 entries

Designed in Massachusetts, built by a Middlebury firm, finished in a single season.

  • Feb 29, 1884Contract signed with Smith & Allen of Middlebury to build the hall for $8,000, to a design by architect C. W. Damon of Haverhill, Massachusetts.
  • Apr 1884Work begins. The foundation and frame are up by the middle of June.
  • Aug 1884Voters approve $3,300 to finish, furnish and seat the building.
  • Nov 1884The hall is finished, at a total cost of $11,233.64 with furnishings. It is 42 by 80 feet, with an upstairs hall seating 500, a large meeting room below and a tower clock bought from the E. Howard Watch and Clock Company for $400. It is named for Winter Holley.

  • 1929Town meeting votes to finish a Town Clerk's office and a records vault in the basement, for about $2,800.
  • 1930Sound equipment is added for talking pictures. In 1932 the movies move across the street to the Colonial Theater.

Offices, classrooms and a landmark

1951 to 1992 · 7 entries

The basement fills with school overflow and the historical society, and the building is recognized as historic.

  • 1951Part of the basement becomes classroom space for overflow students from the Bristol schools, through 1957.
  • 1978The Bristol Historical Society gets 900 square feet of the basement as a meeting place, remodeled by volunteers. It stays until 1991.
  • Feb 1983Holley Hall is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as part of the Bristol Downtown Historic District. The nomination calls it Queen Anne in style with strong Romanesque influence, built of brick veneer on a post-and-beam frame.
  • 1983The clock, clock tower and steeple are restored.
  • 1985The large stained glass windows at the north end of the hall, and the windows on the other sides, are restored.
  • 1991Volunteers build a ramp on the west side so people using wheelchairs can reach the hall.
  • Jul 1992With a $1,025 State Historic Preservation grant, the bell tower roof deck, laid in the 1880s, is replaced.

Renovation and repair

2010 to 2024 · 6 entries

A major renovation, an elevator, new front steps and the energy assessment that led to this project.

  • May 4, 2010The State's historic review finds no adverse effect from a planned renovation: radon work, new first-floor offices, structural repairs to the roof and balcony, a stair and elevator tower on the west side, and stage lighting, a movie screen and a painted curtain.
  • 2010Work runs through 2011. The upstairs hall is restored, the balcony reinforced and the lower level rebuilt with new offices. A south entry addition is built the same year.
  • May 27, 2015FEMA's historic review finds no adverse effect from a propane generator for the building, so it can serve as the Town's emergency operations center. The money comes from hazard mitigation funds after Tropical Storm Irene.
  • 2023The rotted front steps and porch are rebuilt in composite decking, and the concrete landing is repaired that fall.
  • Dec 15, 2023A State Historic Preservation grant request for brick, stained glass and trim repairs is not funded.
  • Jun 18, 2024The energy assessment that led to this project measures how leaky the hall is. The rest of the story is on the Timeline tab.

What was done Upper floor, tower office and lobby

Is the building tighter? Blower door tests, air leakage at 75 pascals

How to read this. A blower door test depressurizes the building with a fan and measures how much air leaks in. Lower is better. The mid-project test was taken before every air barrier was finished. The final test on August 10, 2026, after the work was complete, measured less leakage than before the project. Per square foot of building shell, the hall went from 1.00 cfm, which the 2024 assessment rated "extremely leaky," to 0.42 cfm. The consultant places that in the range of average construction, close to the 0.30 cfm reference Vermont's commercial energy code uses for new buildings.

Testing for PCBs. Before the work, Clay Point Associates screened Holley Hall for PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls) on November 19, 2025. It listed four materials that could contain them: green and white paint on exterior trim, window glazing, and caulking at the windows and doors. All four were in very good condition, so they were not sampled.
